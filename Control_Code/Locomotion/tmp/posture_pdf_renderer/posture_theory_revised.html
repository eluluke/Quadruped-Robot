Overview

Purpose, scope, and central result

What the controller proves, and what it deliberately does not claim

This note derives a kinematic posture-correction layer. An IMU attitude and gyroscope measurement are converted into a geometric attitude error and an angular-rate error. A virtual PD law produces a bounded corrective body twist. For each stance foot, rigid-contact kinematics determine the relative foot velocity compatible with that body motion. The leg Jacobian then maps the correction into joint motion.

IMU stateattitude and gyro
Group errorrelative rotation
Lie logarithmrotation vector
Virtual PDbounded body twist
Contact mapstance-foot velocity
Leg mapJacobian and joints
Central kinematic identity. For stance foot i, with body-frame contact vector r_i^B and commanded body twist (v_c^B,\omega_c^B), stationary no-slip contact requires
\dot r_{i,c}^{B}=-v_c^{B}-\omega_c^{B}\times r_i^{B}.
(1)
For pure attitude correction, set v_c^B=0.

Model boundary

Equation (1) is exact at the velocity-kinematics level under the stated frame and contact assumptions. It does not prove that a position-controlled floating robot will realize a requested body angular acceleration. That stronger statement requires a model of ground-reaction forces, friction, contact switching, body inertia, actuator limits, compliance, and servo dynamics.

Standing assumptions

  • All physical frames are right-handed.
  • R_{WB} maps body coordinates into world coordinates.
  • Quaternion and gyro conventions are mutually consistent.
  • The fixed IMU-to-body mounting rotation is known.
  • A stance foot is stationary and does not slip.
  • Leg frames are rigidly attached to the body.
  • Contact confidence is smoothly gated at phase changes.
  • All commands are freshness-checked and saturated.
Interpretation. The mathematics through the stance-foot velocity map is geometry. The conversion from foot motion to body torque is dynamics. Keeping those layers separate prevents a clean kinematic derivation from being mistaken for a whole-robot stability proof.

Chapter 1

Frames, points, and free vectors

A frame label is part of the mathematical object, not decoration

A physical vector is independent of coordinates, but its numerical components depend on the basis used to describe it. Superscripts identify the coordinate frame. Thus v^B and v^W are two coordinate representations of the same free vector.

SymbolMeaning

p_B^WWorld position of the body-frame origin.
r_i^BVector from the body origin to foot contact i, expressed in \{B\}.

1.1 Point transformation

Let p_{BL_i}^B be the leg-origin position in body coordinates, and let R_{BL_i} map leg coordinates into body coordinates. A foot point transforms by rotation plus translation:

p_{F_i}^{B}=p_{BL_i}^{B}+R_{BL_i}p_{F_i}^{L_i}.
(2)

1.2 Velocity transformation

Because the leg frame is fixed to the body, both p_{BL_i}^B and R_{BL_i} are constant when differentiated in body coordinates. Therefore

\dot p_{F_i}^{B}=R_{BL_i}\dot p_{F_i}^{L_i},\qquad \dot p_{F_i}^{L_i}=R_{BL_i}^{T}\dot p_{F_i}^{B}.
(3)

A velocity is a free vector, so no hip translation is added. This is why a point-position conversion function cannot be reused unchanged for velocity or acceleration.

1.3 Hat and vee operators

a^\wedge=\begin{bmatrix}0&-a_3&a_2\\a_3&0&-a_1\\-a_2&a_1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad a^\wedge b=a\times b,\qquad (a^\wedge)^\vee=a.
(4)
Frame discipline. A cross product is meaningful only after both operands are expressed in the same right-handed frame. A single-axis sign flip has determinant -1; it is a reflection, not a rotation.

Chapter 2

Why rotations form a Lie group

The subtle geometry behind a three-dimensional orientation error

2.1 Group, manifold, and Lie group

Group

A set with composition, identity, inverse, and associativity. Rotation composition is matrix multiplication.

Manifold

A curved space that is locally Euclidean. Near one orientation, a three-vector can serve as a local coordinate.

Lie group

A group that is also a smooth manifold, with smooth composition and inversion.

The special orthogonal group is

SO(3)=\{R\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}\mid R^TR=I,\;\det R=1\}.
(5)

The six orthogonality constraints are not all independent; together with the determinant condition they leave three rotational degrees of freedom. Yet SO(3) is not a vector space: generally, R_1+R_2\notin SO(3). This is the precise reason an attitude error should not be formed by subtracting rotation matrices or quaternions as if they were ordinary coordinates.

2.2 The tangent space and Lie algebra

Consider a differentiable rotation curve R(t). Differentiate the identity R^TR=I:

\dot R^TR+R^T\dot R=0.
(6)

Hence R^T\dot R is skew-symmetric. Every 3\times3 skew-symmetric matrix is the hat image of one vector, so

R^T\dot R=(\omega^B)^\wedge\in\mathfrak{so}(3),\qquad \dot R=R(\omega^B)^\wedge.
(7)

The Lie algebra \mathfrak{so}(3) is the tangent space at the identity:

\mathfrak{so}(3)=\{A\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}\mid A^T=-A\}.
(8)
Vector coordinates
\phi\in\mathbb R^3
→
Lie algebra
\phi^\wedge\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
→
Rotation group
R=\exp(\phi^\wedge)\in SO(3)
Plain-language picture. SO(3) is the curved space of orientations. \mathfrak{so}(3) is the flat tangent space where infinitesimal rotations and angular velocities live. The exponential and logarithm maps move between them locally.

Chapter 2 continued

Exponential and logarithm maps

From a rotation vector to a rotation matrix, and back

2.3 Exponential map and Rodrigues' formula

Let \phi=\theta u, where \|u\|=1. The matrix exponential is defined by its convergent power series:

\exp(\phi^\wedge)=I+\phi^\wedge+\frac{(\phi^\wedge)^2}{2!}+\frac{(\phi^\wedge)^3}{3!}+\cdots.
(9)

Using (u^\wedge)^3=-u^\wedge and (u^\wedge)^4=-(u^\wedge)^2, collect odd and even powers. The sine and cosine series appear, yielding Rodrigues' formula:

\operatorname{Exp}(\phi)=I+\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\phi^\wedge+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}(\phi^\wedge)^2.
(10)

At \theta=0, the coefficients are defined by continuity. For small \phi,

\operatorname{Exp}(\phi)=I+\phi^\wedge+\mathcal O(\|\phi\|^2).
(11)

2.4 Logarithm map

For R\in SO(3), taking the trace of Rodrigues' formula gives \operatorname{tr}(R)=1+2\cos\theta. On the principal branch \theta\in[0,\pi],

\theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(R)-1}{2}\right),\qquad \operatorname{Log}(R)^\vee=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(R-R^T)^\vee.
(12)

Near zero, use a series expansion to avoid division by a small \sin\theta. Near \pi, the rotation axis is sign-ambiguous and numerical extraction needs a dedicated branch.

2.5 Why the logarithm is an error coordinate

If R_e=R^TR_d is the rotation from the measured body frame to the desired body frame, then

e_R=\operatorname{Log}(R_e)^\vee\in\mathbb R^3
(13)

is a minimal local coordinate whose direction is the correction axis and whose norm is the correction angle. It is exact on the selected branch, but it is not a globally unique Euclidean coordinate.

Topological limit. No smooth, globally nonsingular three-parameter attitude coordinate exists for all of SO(3). The logarithm is therefore a local chart with a branch boundary at \theta=\pi, not a magical global subtraction operator.

Chapter 3

Quaternions as a double cover of SO(3)

A compact representation with a sign ambiguity that control code must respect

A unit quaternion is q=(\eta,\epsilon), with scalar part \eta\in\mathbb R, vector part \epsilon\in\mathbb R^3, and \eta^2+\epsilon^T\epsilon=1. An axis-angle rotation has

q=\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2},\;u\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right),\qquad \|u\|=1.
(14)

3.1 Hamilton product and inverse

For q_1=(\eta_1,\epsilon_1) and q_2=(\eta_2,\epsilon_2),

q_1\otimes q_2=\left(\eta_1\eta_2-\epsilon_1^T\epsilon_2,\;\eta_1\epsilon_2+\eta_2\epsilon_1+\epsilon_1\times\epsilon_2\right).
(15)

For a unit quaternion, q^{-1}=q^*=(\eta,-\epsilon). Composition is quaternion multiplication; component-wise subtraction has no invariant rotational meaning.

3.2 Quaternion-to-rotation map

R(q)=(\eta^2-\epsilon^T\epsilon)I+2\epsilon\epsilon^T+2\eta\epsilon^\wedge.
(16)

Replacing q with -q leaves every term in (16) unchanged. Thus q and -q represent the same element of SO(3). This is the double-cover property.

3.3 Relative quaternion and shortest branch

Assume q_{WB} and q_{WD} both map body or desired-frame coordinates into world coordinates. The body-to-desired relative rotation is

q_e=q_{WB}^{-1}\otimes q_{WD},\qquad R(q_e)=R_{WB}^TR_{WD}=R_e.
(17)

Choose the physically equivalent representative with scalar part \eta_e\ge0. If \eta_e<0, negate all four coefficients. Then the principal angle is in [0,\pi], avoiding quaternion unwinding.

3.4 Exact quaternion logarithm

e_R=\begin{cases}2\operatorname{atan2}(\|\epsilon_e\|,\eta_e)\dfrac{\epsilon_e}{\|\epsilon_e\|},&\|\epsilon_e\|>\varepsilon,\\[6pt]2\epsilon_e,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
(18)

The second branch is the small-angle limit, not an arbitrary fallback. Because \sin(\theta/2)=\theta/2+\mathcal O(\theta^3),

2\epsilon_e=\theta u+\mathcal O(\theta^3)=e_R+\mathcal O(\theta^3).
(19)
Implementation rule. Normalize each measured quaternion. For temporal continuity, also flip the new sample if its dot product with the previous sample is negative. That continuity operation is separate from choosing the shortest error branch.

Chapter 4

Error conventions and angular velocity

Left and right errors are both valid, but their coordinates cannot be mixed

4.1 Two invariant relative rotations

Body-resolved error

R_e^B=R^TR_d

Invariant to a common change of world frame. Its logarithm is expressed in the measured body frame.

World-resolved error

R_e^W=R_dR^T

Invariant to a common change of body coordinates. Its logarithm is expressed in the world or desired-side frame.

This guide uses R_e=R^TR_d for the kinematic correction. It points from the measured orientation toward the desired orientation in current body coordinates.

4.2 Rotation-matrix kinematics

For body-resolved angular velocities \omega and \omega_d,

\dot R=R\omega^\wedge,\qquad \dot R_d=R_d\omega_d^\wedge.
(20)

Differentiate R_e=R^TR_d and use \dot R^T=-\omega^\wedge R^T:

\dot R_e=-\omega^\wedge R_e+R_e\omega_d^\wedge.
(21)

Transport desired angular velocity into the measured body frame and define

e_\omega=R_e\omega_d-\omega.
(22)

Because (Ra)^\wedge=Ra^\wedge R^T, equation (21) becomes

\dot R_e=e_\omega^\wedge R_e.
(23)

4.3 Exact derivative of the logarithmic coordinate

Write R_e=\operatorname{Exp}(e_R). Since (23) is a left-trivialized tangent velocity,

e_\omega=J_l(e_R)\dot e_R,\qquad \dot e_R=J_l(e_R)^{-1}e_\omega.
(24)

The left Jacobian of SO(3) is

J_l(\phi)=I+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}\phi^\wedge+\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}(\phi^\wedge)^2,\quad \theta=\|\phi\|.
(25)

Near the identity, J_l(e_R)^{-1}=I-\tfrac12 e_R^\wedge+\mathcal O(\|e_R\|^2). Therefore \dot e_R=e_\omega+\mathcal O(\|e_R\|\|e_\omega\|). This states precisely when the familiar linear error relation is justified.

Fixed desired posture. If \omega_d=0, then e_\omega=-\omega. Raw gyroscope subtraction is valid only after the sensor measurement has been rotated into the same body frame used by the attitude error.

Chapter 5

Virtual PD correction and stability

A rigorous local result, followed by the exact rigid-body controller it approximates

5.1 Virtual angular-acceleration law

Using the body-to-desired errors from Chapter 4, define

\alpha_c=K_Re_R+K_\omega e_\omega,\qquad K_R\succ0,\;K_\omega\succ0.
(26)

Here K_R has units \mathrm{s}^{-2} and K_\omega has units \mathrm{s}^{-1}. For a fixed desired attitude, e_\omega=-\omega.

5.2 Local error dynamics

Assume the physical body realizes \dot\omega=\alpha_c and the error is small enough that J_l(e_R)^{-1}\approx I. Then

\dot e_R=e_\omega,\qquad \dot e_\omega=-K_Re_R-K_\omega e_\omega.
(27)

Choose the positive-definite candidate

V(e_R,e_\omega)=\frac12e_\omega^Te_\omega+\frac12e_R^TK_Re_R.
(28)

Differentiation along (27) gives

\dot V=e_\omega^T(-K_Re_R-K_\omega e_\omega)+e_R^TK_Re_\omega=-e_\omega^TK_\omega e_\omega\le0.
(29)

The cross terms cancel. Locally, the largest invariant subset of \{\dot V=0\} has e_\omega=0 and then K_Re_R=0, hence e_R=0. LaSalle's invariance principle therefore establishes local asymptotic stability of this virtual subsystem.

5.3 Exact torque-level geometric control

For rigid-body torque control, a standard geometric convention defines

E=R_d^TR,\quad e_R^{\mathrm{Lee}}=\frac12(E-E^T)^\vee,\quad e_\Omega=\Omega-R^TR_d\Omega_d.
(30)

With body inertia J, Lee's computed-torque structure is

\tau=-k_Re_R^{\mathrm{Lee}}-k_\Omega e_\Omega+\Omega\times J\Omega-J\!\left(\Omega^\wedge R^TR_d\Omega_d-R^TR_d\dot\Omega_d\right).
(31)
Do not mix signs. Equations (26) and (31) use different error definitions. Their signs are both correct inside their own conventions. Copying one error formula into the other control law without re-derivation can reverse the correction.

Chapter 6

Stationary-contact foot kinematics

The exact velocity and acceleration constraints for a moving body frame

6.1 Position identity

For contact i, let p_{F_i}^W be the world foot position, p_B^W the world body-origin position, and r_i^B the body-to-foot vector in body coordinates:

p_{F_i}^W=p_B^W+R_{WB}r_i^B.
(32)

6.2 Velocity transport theorem

Differentiate (32), using \dot R_{WB}=R_{WB}(\omega^B)^\wedge:

v_{F_i}^W=v_B^W+R_{WB}\left(\omega^B\times r_i^B+\dot r_i^B\right).
(33)

For ideal no-slip stance, v_{F_i}^W=0. Premultiply by R_{WB}^T and define v_B^B=R_{WB}^Tv_B^W:

\dot r_i^B=-v_B^B-\omega^B\times r_i^B.
(34)

The signs are consequences of stationary contact. If the body moves forward over a fixed foot, the foot coordinates move backward relative to the body.

6.3 Exact acceleration constraint

Differentiate (33) in the world frame. A point moving relative to a rotating frame obeys

a_{F_i}^W=a_B^W+R_{WB}\!\left(\alpha^B\times r_i^B+\omega^B\times(\omega^B\times r_i^B)+2\omega^B\times\dot r_i^B+\ddot r_i^B\right).
(35)

For a stationary contact, a_{F_i}^W=0. Defining a_B^B=R_{WB}^Ta_B^W gives

\ddot r_i^B=-a_B^B-\alpha^B\times r_i^B-\omega^B\times(\omega^B\times r_i^B)-2\omega^B\times\dot r_i^B.
(36)

Equation (36) exposes the terms that disappear in a low-speed approximation: centrifugal acceleration and the Coriolis term caused by relative leg motion. At low angular speed with v_B^B=0, the leading relation is \ddot r_i^B\approx-\alpha^B\times r_i^B.

Recommended realization. Integrate one bounded corrective body angular velocity, then recompute every stance-foot velocity from (34). Do not independently integrate four foot accelerations; those states can drift away from any single rigid-body motion.

Chapter 7

From body correction to joint commands

Leg-frame transformation, resolved-rate control, and damped inversion

7.1 Body-to-leg velocity

Transform the body-frame stance correction with rotation only:

\dot p_{F_i,c}^{L_i}=R_{BL_i}^T\dot r_{i,c}^{B}=R_{BL_i}^T\left(-v_c^B-\omega_c^B\times r_i^B\right).
(37)

The hip-origin translation belongs to point positions, not free velocities. Each R_{BL_i} must satisfy R_{BL_i}^TR_{BL_i}=I and \det R_{BL_i}=1.

7.2 Differential leg kinematics

For leg joints q_i\in\mathbb R^3 and forward kinematics p_i^{L_i}=f_i(q_i),

\dot p_i^{L_i}=J_i(q_i)\dot q_i,\qquad J_i(q_i)=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial q_i}.
(38)

A robust resolved-rate command combines desired task velocity with task-space position feedback:

\dot q_{i,c}=J_i^{\#}\!\left[\dot p_{i,d}^{L_i}+K_x\left(p_{i,d}^{L_i}-p_i^{L_i}\right)\right].
(39)

The bracketed quantity has units of velocity: K_x has units \mathrm{s}^{-1}. The Jacobian therefore converts one task-space velocity into joint velocity; it is not mixing position and velocity dimensions.

7.3 Damped least-squares inverse

J_i^{\#}=J_i^T\left(J_iJ_i^T+\lambda^2I\right)^{-1},\qquad \lambda>0.
(40)

This is the minimizer of the regularized least-squares problem

\min_{\dot q_i}\;\|J_i\dot q_i-\dot p_{i,c}\|^2+\lambda^2\|\dot q_i\|^2.
(41)

The damping term prevents joint velocity from diverging near a singular configuration, at the price of task-space tracking bias.

7.4 Position-only motor interface

If firmware consumes position targets but no velocity feedforward, integrate with measured loop time:

q_{i,c}[k+1]=q_{i,c}[k]+\operatorname{sat}_{\dot q}\!\left(\dot q_{i,c}[k]\right)\Delta t_k.
(42)
Drift warning. Integrating joint velocity creates state. Anchor the resolved-rate correction to the current desired foot trajectory and measured foot position as in (39), reset safely at mode changes, and clamp the integrated command to joint and workspace limits.

Controller construction

A mathematically consistent discrete-time contract

1
Read synchronized state. Acquire timestamped quaternion q_{WB}, body gyro \omega^B, joint positions, and contact confidence c_i\in[0,1]. Reject stale data.
2
Normalize and align frames. Normalize the quaternion and apply the fixed sensor-to-body rotation to attitude and gyro.
3
Build the group error. Compute R_e=R_{WB}^TR_{WD} or the equivalent q_e=q_{WB}^{-1}\otimes q_{WD}.
4
Choose the branch. Select the quaternion representative with nonnegative scalar part, then compute the exact log vector e_R.
5
Transport rate. Compute e_\omega=R_e\omega_d-\omega. For level hold, e_\omega=-\omega.
6
Virtual correction. Evaluate and saturate \alpha_c=K_Re_R+K_\omega e_\omega.
7
Bounded integration. Update a leaky corrective angular velocity:
\omega_c[k+1]=\operatorname{sat}_{\omega}\!\left((1-\gamma\Delta t_k)\omega_c[k]+\alpha_c[k]\Delta t_k\right),\qquad \gamma\ge0.
(43)
8
Contact-aware foot command. Blend correction only into stance feet:
\dot r_{i,cmd}^{B}=\dot r_{i,gait}^{B}+c_i\left(-v_c^B-\omega_c^B\times r_i^B\right).
(44)
9
Map to joints. Rotate into \{L_i\}, apply (39)-(40), limit joint rates and accelerations, then integrate position targets.
10
Supervise. Stop on stale feedback, CAN faults, impossible quaternion norm, workspace violation, excessive tracking error, or user emergency input.

Mode-transition rules

Entering correction

Initialize \omega_c=0; ramp contact weights and gains; preserve the current nominal gait phase.

Leaving correction

Ramp correction to zero before resetting integrators; keep the nominal trajectory continuous.

Roll and pitch first. For early hardware tests, set yaw gain to zero. Magnetometer disturbances and heading drift can make yaw correction fight a mechanically sound gait, while roll and pitch are directly observable from gravity-referenced attitude fusion.

Chapter 9

Why kinematics alone cannot guarantee posture

Ground-reaction forces are the physical actuator of floating-body motion

9.1 Centroidal Newton-Euler dynamics

Let p_C be the center of mass, m the total mass, f_i the stance-foot forces, and \tau_i any contact moments. Then

m\ddot p_C=mg+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}f_i,
(45)
\dot h_C=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}(p_{F_i}-p_C)\times f_i+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\tau_i.
(46)

The stance set \mathcal S changes with gait phase. A swing foot has no ground-reaction force and cannot directly create body wrench.

9.2 Contact feasibility

A unilateral point contact must satisfy

f_{i,z}\ge0,\qquad \sqrt{f_{i,x}^2+f_{i,y}^2}\le\mu_i f_{i,z}.
(47)

If the requested horizontal force exceeds the friction cone, the foot slips no matter how accurate the posture kinematics are. If the center of pressure or feasible contact wrench cannot create the requested moment, the body tilts.

9.3 What a position servo actually realizes

When a joint position controller moves a stance leg against the ground, contact force emerges through closed-chain position error, gearbox stiffness and backlash, structural compliance, tire or floor deformation, motor saturation, and controller bandwidth. Therefore the map

\dot r_{i,cmd}\longmapsto f_i\longmapsto(\ddot p_C,\dot h_C)
(48)

is state-dependent and generally unknown. The virtual acceleration \alpha_c is a shaping signal, not a measured body acceleration command.

Consequent claim. The Lyapunov proof in Chapter 5 certifies the ideal virtual error dynamics under the assumption \dot\omega=\alpha_c. It does not certify the complete quadruped with position servos and intermittent contact.

9.4 Path toward force-based control

A future whole-body or MPC layer can choose feasible stance forces that realize a desired centroidal wrench, then map them to joint torque by \tau_i=J_i(q_i)^Tf_i. Until the firmware and sensing support that layer reliably, the kinematic correction should remain slow, bounded, stance-gated, and experimentally identified.

Chapter 10

Discrete implementation and numerical safety

The sampled controller is part of the plant

10.1 Use measured loop time

Let timestamps be generated by a monotonic clock. Use

\Delta t_k=t_k-t_{k-1}
(49)

in every integration. Clamp or reject implausible \Delta t_k. A nominal 1/f_{loop} is appropriate for planning, but substituting it for measured time under CAN or scheduler jitter causes integration drift.

10.2 Filter what is noisy, not what is geometric

  • Normalize the quaternion, but do not independently low-pass its four components without re-normalization.
  • Use the BNO055 fused orientation as orientation; use the gyro as angular rate. Do not numerically differentiate quaternion samples to recreate a noisier gyro.
  • Low-pass gyro bias and high-frequency noise only enough to preserve posture-control phase margin.
  • Do not depend on double integration of consumer-IMU acceleration for global body position.

10.3 Saturation hierarchy

11.1 Project-specific frame audit

The current project must explicitly document the axis directions of the body frame, all four leg frames, the IMU sensor frame, positive joint rotations, and whether foot z increases upward or downward. A mapping that adds body offsets is valid for points. A mapping that flips only one axis is a reflection and cannot be used as a right-handed rotation for angular quantities or cross products.

11.2 Numerical diagnostic plots

Before hardware, plot e_R, e_\omega, \alpha_c, \omega_c, each contact weight, each body-frame foot correction, Jacobian condition number, and final joint command. A sign error should be visible in a one-axis test before it becomes a body motion.

Near-pi test. The log map is least numerically comfortable near 180^\circ. The hardware posture layer should never be expected to recover a fallen robot through that branch; reject or separately handle such large errors.

Contact blending. Enable stance weights at slow stepping speed. Confirm no command jump at liftoff or touchdown.
6
Walking experiment. Compare baseline gait and corrected gait using repeatable floor, battery voltage, payload, and initial pose. Measure improvement rather than judging one run.

12.1 Minimum telemetry

  • Monotonic timestamp and measured \Delta t
  • Raw and normalized quaternion
  • Body-frame gyro
  • e_R,e_\omega,\alpha_c,\omega_c
  • Gait phase and contact weights
  • Desired and measured foot positions
  • Jacobian singular values or condition number
  • Desired and measured joint positions
  • Tracking-error and saturation flags
  • CAN/PDO age and motor fault state

12.2 What success means

For this first layer, success is not dynamic optimality. Success means that small roll and pitch disturbances produce correctly signed, bounded, contact-aware leg corrections; the corrections reduce attitude error without increasing slip, tracking error, or mode-transition impacts; and every limit or stale-data event reaches a deterministic safe state.

Engineering conclusion. The posture layer is a bridge between open-loop foot trajectories and future whole-body control. It is valuable precisely because its geometric portion can be verified independently, while its unmodeled contact realization is kept experimentally modest and visibly bounded.

12.3 Future extension

Once force sensing, actuator characterization, and state estimation are trustworthy, equations (45)-(47) can become constraints in a force-distribution quadratic program or MPC. That future controller would choose contact forces directly instead of hoping that foot-position error creates the desired wrench.

Notation statement

Superscripts denote the frame in which vector components are expressed. Subscripts identify objects or mappings. The hat map sends vectors to skew-symmetric matrices; the vee map is its inverse. \operatorname{Exp} and \operatorname{Log} denote the SO(3) exponential and principal logarithm. Quaternion products use the Hamilton convention, and all rotation mappings are stated explicitly before use.

Final result

The attitude error must be formed by group composition and mapped into the tangent space before PD feedback. The stance-foot correction follows from a stationary-contact constraint, and body-to-leg conversion uses rotation only for velocities. These results are mathematically rigorous within their kinematic assumptions. The remaining question - whether the physical body realizes the requested motion - belongs to contact dynamics, actuator behavior, and state estimation.

One-line controller contract. Geometry computes a consistent correction; contact and actuator dynamics decide whether the robot can realize it.
